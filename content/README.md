### Scouting + strategy / every competition

**Picking our alliance for the playoffs.**

Qualification rankings are close to meaningless because the match schedule is random. So the real work is scouting: watching every robot at the event, recording what it actually does, and turning that into a ranked list of who you want as a partner in the playoffs.

I ran this my junior and senior year. I built the scouting apps our stand scouts used, ran the data collection, blended it with public analytics like OPR and EPA, and led the picklist meeting where we argued robots head to head until the order was right. Then I took the list to the field and made the picks.

I also taught it. New scouts learned the system from me, and other teams asked how we did it, so I walked them through it too.

1. **Collect.** Scouts in the stands logging every robot every match: pieces scored in auto and teleop, cycle times, climb success, plus notes on driver skill and how fast a team fixes a broken robot in the pits.
2. **Build the tools.** I designed the scouting apps that captured all of it, so the data came back clean instead of as a stack of paper forms nobody could read.
3. **Define what wins.** Before ranking anyone, decide what a winning alliance looks like for that year's game. That gives you a separate first pick list and second pick list, because the two picks do different jobs.
4. **Sort and argue.** Tier the field, order each tier by composite score, then go head to head: would we rather play with A or B? Match video and breakdown history settle it. A do-not-pick list comes out of the same meeting.
5. **Pick.** Take the list to the field, cross off teams as other captains take them, and draft the highest name left. You get about a minute, so the thinking has to already be done.

*CVR 2025 / RAW SCOUTING DATA BEFORE SORTING. One row per team at the event: average auto points, endgame, teleop by level, and net points.*

### In focus / 2025 end effector: seven iterations in five weeks

The 2025 game used two pieces: PVC pipes called coral and rubber balls called algae. Our end effector had to pick up and hold both, from the station and off the floor. I led it, designed it in Onshape, and took it through seven versions in five weeks.

Funnel angle and compression decided everything. Too wide and the coral would not index, too tight and it knocked pieces off the floor. Star wheels helped the coral and hurt the algae, so they came out and a centering spike went in. The 3D printed gearbox cover kept breaking during drive practice, so the gearbox moved inside the structure and the polycarbonate frame became self-reinforced polypropylene to save weight.

It held up through three regionals and the World Championship. Division Finalist, our best finish since 2019, plus the Excellence in Engineering Award.

## 002 / Mandarin Learning Website

EDTECH · PRODUCT · UI · 2025

I took four years of Mandarin class. I could read and write almost fluently but I still couldn't follow a conversation.

IMPORT YOUR OWN VOCABULARY LIST. THE SITE BUILDS LISTENING PRACTICE OUT OF THE WORDS YOU HAVE ACTUALLY BEEN TAUGHT.

Example list (UNIT 7 / SCHOOL, 6 words): 学校 xuéxiào school · 老师 lǎoshī teacher · 朋友 péngyou friend · 喜欢 xǐhuan to like · 图书馆 túshūguǎn library · 考试 kǎoshì test

### The gap

**Reading Mandarin and understanding it out loud turned out to be really different.**

Class taught me to read and write. Nobody practiced listening with me. And every listening tool I found used random words I'd never been taught, so I couldn't tell if I was bad at listening or just hadn't learned the words yet.

- ON PAPER: 我的朋友喜欢学校的图书馆。 (wǒ de péngyou xǐhuan xuéxiào de túshūguǎn, MY FRIEND LIKES THE SCHOOL LIBRARY.) I could read this sentence without any trouble.
- OUT LOUD, AT FULL SPEED: When someone said it at normal speed, I couldn't pick out the words.

### Why it has to be personal

**Students will use something if it helps with what they're learning in class.**

In a classroom, most students' goal is to get a better grade. If a tool teaches words that aren't going to be on the test, it feels like extra homework and people stop using it pretty quickly.

If the practice uses the same vocab list the class is already studying, then studying for the test and practicing listening end up being the same thing. Students have a reason to use it because it helps their grade, and they get better at listening along the way.

- A GENERIC LISTENING TOOL: 今天的天气很好，我们坐飞机去北京. Three of these words hadn't come up in class yet, so if I got it wrong I couldn't tell if it was my listening or just words I hadn't learned.
- BUILT FROM MY CLASS LIST: 我的朋友喜欢学校的图书馆. Every word here is one we had already covered in class, so if I got it wrong I knew it was my listening, which is what I actually wanted to practice.

### How it works: IMPORT → GENERATE → CHECK → LISTEN

1. **Import.** You can paste in a list, type it in manually, or use the words from a textbook unit.
2. **Generate.** The sentences only use words from your list, plus really basic words like 我 and 的. (Examples: 老师说明天有考试。 / 我的朋友在图书馆。)
3. **Check.** Each sentence gets checked for grammar and for words that aren't on the list before anyone hears it. (✕ 我的朋友喜欢图书馆学校。 WORD ORDER · ✕ 朋友在图书馆喝咖啡。 咖啡 IS NOT ON YOUR LIST · ✓ 我的朋友喜欢学校的图书馆。)
4. **Listen.** You hear the sentence first without seeing the characters, then pick what it means. (WHAT DID YOU HEAR? The teacher says there is a test tomorrow. / My friend likes the school library. / The teacher is at the library today.)

**Gets harder as you go.** LV1 考试。 → LV2 明天有考试。 → LV3 老师说明天有考试。 When you get a few right in a row, the sentences get longer, and if you miss a few it goes back to shorter ones. Yasen built this part so it stays at a level that feels challenging but not frustrating.

### The psychology behind it

**How it feels to practice affects how much you learn.**

A lot of learning a language comes down to confidence. When I was nervous about getting something wrong I would stop really listening and just guess, and when I felt like I was getting it I wanted to keep going.

Because of that I spent a lot of time on how the site responds when you answer. Most of these choices come from research on how people learn languages.

- WHEN YOU GET IT RIGHT (Correct ★★★): When you get it right, the answer turns green and you get stars right away. Seeing that you got it makes you want to try another one.
- WHEN YOU MISS IT (Not quite ✕): When you get it wrong, it turns red and shows you what the sentence actually said, and then you move on to the next one.

| # | Principle | The idea | On the site |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 01 | Understandable input | People learn a language best from things they can mostly understand already. | Every sentence uses words from your class list, so you already know almost all of it. |
| 02 | One step harder | You learn the most when something is a little harder than what you already know, but not so hard that you give up. | The sentences get longer as you get them right and shorter when you miss. |
| 03 | Instant feedback | Finding out right away whether you were right helps it stick better. | Your answer turns green or red as soon as you pick it. |
| 04 | Small rewards | Small rewards make people more likely to keep practicing. | You get stars every time you get one right. |
| 05 | Low stress | Being anxious makes it harder to take in a new language, so it helps when mistakes don't feel like a big deal. | A wrong answer just turns red and shows you the right sentence. |
| 06 | Recall, not reread | Trying to remember a word helps you learn it more than just looking at it again. | You hear the sentence before you see any characters. |

### How I built it (set against Duolingo's operating principles)

1. **Learners first.** This started as my own problem in Mandarin class. I tried to design it around what students actually want in a class, which is usually a better grade, so the listening practice comes along with that.
2. **Test it first.** A lot of the first sentences the AI wrote were wrong, so we added a filter that checks every one, and James went through them as a native speaker before students saw them.
3. **Reduce complexity.** The only thing you have to give it is your vocab list. There is no placement test or course to pick.
4. **Take the long view.** The list keeps growing as the class moves through the year, so it stays useful later on and not just for the first unit.
5. **Ship it.** Teachers added it to the curriculum, so anyone at my high school can use it now.

**BUILD + TEAM.** I created a platform where you can import your own vocabulary list, from a textbook or typed in manually, and it generates practice sentences using only those words. I built the platform and put the pieces together. My friend James checked the Mandarin as a native speaker. My friend Yasen helped with the backend and the logic that adjusts difficulty as you go.

**RESULT.** The AI kept writing sentences that were wrong, so we added a validation filter. Every sentence gets checked before you see it. Teachers added the site to the curriculum, so anyone at my high school can use it now.

### If I kept going: what I'd build next

- A. The teacher uploads the unit list once and the whole class gets it.
- B. Practice that lines up with when the quiz is, so there's more of it the week before the test.
- C. Speaking practice, where you say the sentence back and it tells you which tones you got wrong.

## 003 / Spartan Robotics Camp

EDUCATION · BUSINESS · LEADERSHIP · 2024

A one-week LEGO SPIKE Prime camp for grades 3–8, written as a business plan and pitched to a sports camp that had never taught STEM.

![Campers watching an FRC robot demonstration in the shop](images/p4-spartan-robotics-camp/CAMP_HERO.jpg)

### The numbers (three summers / 2024–2026)

- **$55,473+** profit across the first two summers, all of it funding Team 971
- **BIGGEST** STEM camp run at a high school in the Bay Area
- **SOLD OUT** all three summers, every session, starting with 45 campers in year one

**CONTEXT.** Spartans Sports Camp is a 501(c)(3) affiliated with Mountain View High School athletics. It was trusted and well run, but had no STEM offering. Bay Area robotics camps were charging $525–599 a week. Rohan Gadde and I wrote a business plan for a student-led robotics program under their brand and pitched it to the director.

**WHAT I DESIGNED.** I designed the curriculum, budget, staffing, and daily structure. Three days of building and programming challenges, then two days of competition we called Robo-Olympics. Twenty LEGO SPIKE Prime kits, one per two campers, in the MVHS engineering and computer science rooms. Ten FRC Team 971 counselors a week plus two paid adult supervisors. Live demos with our 125-lb FRC robot, which the kids get to touch.

**RESULT.** Year one sold out: one week, 45 campers, $19,383 profit on a volunteer staff. Year two split into beginner and advanced sessions and cleared $36,090 with paid staff. Second-highest revenue of the camp's 13 programs, and the profit funded my high school robotics team. A third summer ran in 2026 after I graduated. Final numbers are still coming in. I ran interviews, payroll, and operations, and brought the robot to community events to fill seats.

## 004 / Festival of Lights Floats

FABRICATION · SOUND · LIGHTING · 2026

![Illuminated music float with organ pipes and toy soldiers at night](images/p8-festival-of-lights-floats/FOL_HERO.jpg)

I work for the City of Los Altos on the floats for the Festival of Lights parade. I build them, design them, and run the sound and lighting on them. It is the parade I grew up watching in my own hometown.

**THE PARADE.** The Los Altos Festival of Lights Parade has run since 1977, when a few downtown merchants pulled together four or five push floats for a small parade. It is now an all-volunteer association that shuts down State and Main the Sunday after Thanksgiving, and the floats live year-round in a warehouse at the city corporation yard.

**WHAT I DO.** Construction and design, plus the sound and lighting engineering. Some floats are push floats. Others are fully modified vehicles with an engine, a drivetrain, and everything that comes with keeping one running. Every year we do maintenance on the very old floats, refurbish what needs it, and design a brand new float from scratch.

**CLEAN ENERGY.** We have been converting as many floats as possible to battery power. The old systems were not clean energy, and swapping them out means quieter floats, no exhaust in a crowd of kids, and a lot of rewiring per float.

## 006 / Lissajous Darkroom Prints

SOUND · PHYSICS · PHOTOGRAPHY · 2025

Two perpendicular waves draw a curve that disappears as soon as the sound stops. I exposed six songs onto photo paper with a laser so the shapes stayed.

SOURCE / SOUND · PRINTS / 006 · PROCESS / DARKROOM · EXPOSURE / TRIAL + ERROR

### The curves, live

watch this video to see more :D

[![Lissajous curves drawn by a laser](images/p3-lissajous-darkroom-prints/VIDEO_POSTER.jpg)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CmFj3oJCAFU)

![Diagram of the rig: sound into a tube, a balloon membrane with a mirror, a laser bouncing off it onto a screen](images/p3-lissajous-darkroom-prints/RIG_DIAGRAM.jpg)

**HOW THE RIG WORKS: Sound moves a mirror, and the mirror steers the laser.** A speaker pushes air down a tube into a balloon stretched over the end. A small mirror glued to the membrane vibrates with the audio, and a laser aimed at that mirror throws the deflection onto the wall, or in the darkroom, onto photo paper.

**OBSERVATION.** I was experimenting with sound visualization using a balloon, a speaker and a laser (you should try it) and got caught by Lissajous curves, the pattern two perpendicular waves draw when they interact. They were genuinely one of the most mesmerizing things I had ever seen, so I set out on a quest to capture them.

**METHOD.** I chose six songs with different harmonic structures, so each one draws a different pattern. In the darkroom I exposed photo paper to a laser influenced by a different portion of each song, so the sound became six physical prints.

**REFLECTION.** Exposure times took a lot of trial and error. The physics is easy enough to look up. Getting a print that looks like what you see on the wall was the hard part.

## 007 / CRT TV Guitar

OBJECT · MUSIC · EXPERIMENT · 2025

A CRT television, an Arduino, and a fretless ribbon neck cut from birch. Sound comes out of the TV speaker; the notes show up as bars on the screen.

![CRT TV guitar being played](images/p2-crt-tv-guitar/CRT_HERO.jpg)

[![CRT TV Guitar being played (video)](images/p2-crt-tv-guitar/VIDEO_POSTER.jpg)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eO8PTNAAx4I)

| | |
| --- | --- |
| MATERIALS | CRT TV / Arduino / ribbon pot / birch |
| ROLE | Design, fabrication, electronics |
| OUTPUT | Sound + image, one signal |
| STATUS | Functioning, played live |

**CONTEXT.** I have always been drawn to analog technology and think it is too cool not to be properly appreciated. I saw a clip from the band Electronicos Fantasticos! and was inspired to build my own CRT TV guitar.

**BUILD.** The Arduino reads voltage off a ribbon potentiometer as my finger slides along it. Each position maps to a frequency. RCA cables I soldered feed an RF modulator, so the signal becomes both audio and image. The neck is birch, cut on a vertical bandsaw and varnished.

**RESULT.** The ribbon pot works like a fretless neck, so I can slide between notes and add vibrato. It plays more like an otamatone than a guitar. In the video I play "In My Life" by the Beatles. I couldn't decide what song to learn, and that's what my grandma suggested.
